This interactive lab has six parts and two closing tabs. Part A combines the five modality probabilities of a patient of the chest pain unit by late fusion, and Part B computes the attention of a class token over five modality tokens with a mask. Part C judges and updates the calibration of the fusion model of the course at two hospitals, and Part D moves a threshold for discharge over the test patients next to HEART 0 to 3. Part E is a drill on the review of a fusion pipeline, and Part F is the prompt workshop. The tab Check yourself holds a self-assessment, and the last tab a reflection and the learning log to export.

Part A. Late fusion by hand

Choose a test patient of the chest pain unit, or set the five probabilities yourself. The table gives for every modality its probability of MACE, its logit and its weight in the stacking of the course. The mean of the logits gives every modality the same say; stacking adds the weighted logits to its intercept. Untick a modality to leave it out: The mean then averages the others, and stacking uses the mean logit of the test patients for the missing modality.

For CP-3304, the mean of the five logits gives 0.393 and stacking 0.884. Why do the two differ so much?

The mean divides the sum of the five logits by 5, so two modalities that point low pull the result toward them, whatever the troponin says. Stacking learned on the validation set that the troponin deserves the largest weight (0.844) and that the evidence of several modalities adds up; its intercept, 2.485, corrects for the base risk that each logit carries. The calibration slope of the mean of the logits, 2.78, says the same: Its logits are far too small.

The echocardiogram of a patient is masked. What happens to the ratio between the weights of the troponin and the ECG?

The weight of each present modality is its exponential divided by the sum over the present modalities. Masking one term changes only the denominator, which is the same for all, so the ratios between the present modalities stay as they were.

Part C. Calibration and recalibration

The reliability curve of early fusion: The patients are sorted by their predicted risk and cut into ten groups of equal size, and the mean predicted risk of each group is drawn against its observed share of MACE. Choose the patients, then shift the logits by a and stretch them by a factor b: Every probability becomes 1/(1 + e^−(a + b·logit p)). For the changed probabilities, the panel gives the mean predicted risk, the observed share, the Brier score, the calibration intercept and slope, and the AUC, which does not change.

At hospital B, early fusion predicts a mean risk of about 25 percent while 31 percent of the patients have a MACE. Which change of the logits brings the curve to the diagonal?

A model that underestimates the risk has a positive calibration intercept, and the shift that removes it is positive. Hospital B learned a shift of 0.37 on its first 300 patients. The shift moves the whole curve up and leaves the order of the patients, and so the AUC, unchanged. A stretch b changes the slope of the curve, which is needed when the probabilities are too extreme or too timid.

Part D. A threshold for discharge

Every test patient is a point at its predicted risk of MACE, red when a MACE followed. Patients at or below the threshold go home. Move the threshold, or use one of the two thresholds chosen on the validation set, and read how many go home, how many of them have a MACE and the Wilson interval of that share, the sensitivity and the net benefit of admitting the patients above the threshold. HEART 0 to 3 is given for the same patients.

With the threshold that sends home as many validation patients as HEART 0 to 3, the model sends home 388 test patients, 9 of whom have a MACE, and HEART 0 to 3 sends home 380, 8 of whom have a MACE. What can the heart team conclude?

A difference of one or two missed events among some 380 patients sent home is well inside the play of chance: The Wilson intervals of the two rules overlap almost completely, and the interval of the difference in the same bootstrap samples contains 0. To show that one rule is safer would take thousands of patients. The threshold is never chosen on the test patients, because the result would then be too good.

Part E. Fusion pipeline drill

Each example below is part of a fusion pipeline written by an AI assistant, or a prompt sent to one. Read it with the seven questions of the review of a fusion pipeline and choose its hidden problem.

1.

features = ["age", "heart_rate", "troponin_0h", "troponin_1h", "troponin_3h", "lvef"]
# model for the decision one hour after arrival
The troponin after three hours does not exist one hour after arrival, when the decision is taken. A model trained with it looks better than any model that can be used at the bedside, and it fails when the value is not there. Every input needs a time stamp before the index time.

2.

X = pd.get_dummies(unit[["age", "sex", "troponin_0h", "ecg_reading", "diagnosis"]])
model.fit(X[train], y[train])   # AUC 0.99
The final diagnosis, NSTEMI or STEMI, is written after the work-up and almost defines the outcome: Every myocardial infarction is a MACE. The AUC of 0.99 measures the diagnosis, not the risk at arrival. Columns written after the decision, such as the diagnosis, the type of event or the length of stay, never enter a model.

3.

tfidf = TfidfVectorizer().fit(unit["note_tr"])          # all 4,000 notes
X = tfidf.transform(unit["note_tr"])
train, test = X[:3200], X[3200:]
The words, their weights and every reduction of the notes are learned from the test notes as well, before the split. The test set then shaped the model, and its result is too good. The text model, the scaling and the filling of missing values are learned on the training set and only applied to the others.

4.

for t in np.arange(0.005, 0.10, 0.005):
    print(t, missed(y_test, p_test <= t), (p_test <= t).mean())
threshold = 0.06                       # most patients home with at most 2 percent missed, on the test set
A threshold tuned on the test patients fits their chance events, and the share sent home and the events missed reported at it are too good. The threshold is chosen on the validation set, and the test set is used once, at the end.

5.

Report: "The fusion model predicts MACE with an AUC of 0.921 and can be used to tell patients their risk."
An AUC says that the patients are ranked well, not that the probabilities mean what they say. The mean of the logits of the course has exactly this AUC and a calibration slope of 2.8: The risks it would tell patients are far from the truth. A model that gives probabilities to patients is reported with its calibration intercept, slope, Brier score and reliability curve.

6.

both = pd.concat([hospital_a, hospital_b])
both["troponin"] = both["troponin_ng_per_l"]       # hs-cTnT at A, hs-cTnI at B
Troponin T at A and troponin I at B have different upper reference limits, 14 and 26 ng/L, so the same number means different things. A value of 30 ng/L is twice the limit at A and barely above it at B. Each value is expressed in multiples of the limit of its own assay, or the model is built and checked for each assay.

7.

Prompt: Here are the triage notes of my last 50 patients with chest pain, with their names and phone numbers for follow-up. Group them by the character of the pain and estimate the risk of each patient.
Notes with names and telephone numbers are personal health data of a special category under the KVKK, and they do not go to a service outside the institution. The assistant needs the meaning of the columns and the form of the notes, which can be described in words or shown with invented examples.

Part F. Prompt workshop

On the prompt path, a prompt takes the place of the code. Choose an exercise of the Jupyter notebook, write the seven fields of the prompt card in clinical language and watch the quality meter and the privacy check. The prompt names only the table "data" that the exercise cell prepares and the result "answer". Then copy the prompt into an AI assistant, paste the code it returns into the exercise cell in place of the line answer = None, and let the last line of the cell check the result.

    A weak and a strong prompt for the same task
    Both prompts ask for the late fusion of the echocardiogram and the laboratory values for heart failure events. The code under each prompt is an example of what an AI assistant may return.
    Weak prompt
    combine the echo model and the lab model and tell me how good it is
    Example of code an assistant may return:
    p = (data["probability, echocardiogram"] + data["probability, laboratory"]) / 2
pred = p > 0.5
print("Accuracy:", (pred == data["event"]).mean())          # 0.948
    Strong prompt, with the prompt card
    Role: You are helping a cardiologist who does not write code. The code will be pasted into a Jupyter notebook and run there.
Context: The cardiologist wants to know how well the echocardiogram and the laboratory values predict heart failure events together, by averaging the opinions of their two models.
Data: The notebook holds a table named "data" with the test patients of the registry of the virtual clinic, a synthetic cohort. Each row is a patient: three columns give the probability of death or a hospitalisation for heart failure within 24 months from three models of the course, each trained on one modality: "probability, ECG", "probability, echocardiogram" and "probability, laboratory"; "event" is 1 when such an event followed and 0 otherwise.
Task: Turn the probabilities of the echocardiogram and of the laboratory into logits, log(p / (1 − p)), take the mean of the two logits for every patient and compute its area under the ROC curve (AUC) against the column "event".
Constraints: Use only this table. The code must not read files, download anything from the internet or install programs.
Output: Print the AUC, and keep it, with three decimals, under the name "answer".
Test: The AUC must lie between 0 and 1.
    Example of code an assistant may return:
    p = np.clip(data[["probability, echocardiogram", "probability, laboratory"]].to_numpy(), 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)
z = np.log(p / (1 - p)).mean(axis=1)
answer = round(float(roc_auc_score(data["event"], z)), 3)
print("AUC of the late fusion:", answer)

# test: the AUC lies between 0 and 1
print(0 <= answer <= 1)
    Which fields of the strong prompt prevent the errors of the weak one?
    The weak code averages probabilities instead of logits and reports an accuracy at a threshold of 0.5. With about 5 percent of the test patients having an event, a model that calls no one at risk is already 95 percent accurate, so the number says nothing. The strong prompt names the columns and their meaning, asks for the mean of the logits, the late fusion of the lecture, and for the AUC, and its test checks the result.

    Check yourself
    Seven questions for self-assessment. They are not graded.
    1. Early fusion is built from a clinical table of 12 values, troponin of 4 values and an echocardiogram of 3 values. How many columns does its row have?
    The numbers of the three modalities are put side by side: 12 + 4 + 3 = 19 columns, one row per patient.
    2. Three modality models give a patient the logits −1, 0 and 2. What does late fusion by the mean of the logits give as a probability?
    The mean is (−1 + 0 + 2)/3 = 0.333, and 1/(1 + e^−0.333) = 0.583.
    3. A class token gives three modalities the scores 0, 0 and 0, and the third modality is masked. What are the weights of the first two?
    Equal scores give equal weights over the present modalities: e⁰/(e⁰ + e⁰) = 0.5 each. The masked modality gets 0.
    4. A model predicts a mean risk of 10 percent in a hospital where 20 percent of the patients have the event. What is the sign of its calibration intercept there?
    The intercept is the shift that makes the mean predicted risk equal to the observed share. A model that predicts too little needs an upward shift: a positive intercept.
    5. A model gives every one of four patients the risk 0.5; two of them have the event. What is its Brier score?
    Every patient contributes (0.5 − 1)² or (0.5 − 0)², both 0.25, so the mean is 0.25: the score of a model that knows only the share of events.
    6. At a threshold probability of 20 percent, a rule admits 100 of 1,000 patients: 40 with an event and 60 without. What is its net benefit?
    Net benefit = 40/1,000 − 60/1,000 × 0.20/0.80 = 0.040 − 0.015 = 0.025.
    7. A hospital updates the intercept of a model with its own first patients. What happens to the AUC of the model on its later patients?
    Adding the same shift to every logit keeps the order of the patients, and the AUC depends only on that order. The update improves calibration, not discrimination.

    Weekly project
    What to do. Build a model for major adverse cardiac events (MACE) within 30 days from the five modalities of the chest pain unit and judge it as a heart team would before using it. First, fit a logistic regression on each modality alone and one on all of them together (early fusion), with the regularisation chosen on the validation set, and give their test AUCs with 95 percent intervals from 2,000 bootstrap samples of the test patients, together with the difference of each to the HEART score in the same samples. Second, judge the calibration of early fusion on the test set by its intercept, its slope, its Brier score and a reliability curve of ten groups; then do the same at hospital B for the 700 patients after its first 300, before and after an update of the intercept learned on the first 300. Third, choose two thresholds for discharge on the validation set, one that sends home as many patients as HEART 0 to 3 and one that keeps the sensitivity at 98 percent, and report for each, on the test set and at hospital B, the share discharged and the MACE among the discharged with Wilson intervals, next to HEART 0 to 3. Fourth, draw the decision curve of the model and of the rule "admit every patient with HEART 4 or more" for threshold probabilities from 1 to 30 percent, and write half a page for a heart team on whether the model should be used, for which decision and with what local check.
