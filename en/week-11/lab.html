This interactive lab has six parts and two closing tabs. Part A is the canvas of a final project: the structured question with its checks, collected in one block for the proposal. Part B computes how much data a model needs with the three criteria of Riley and colleagues. Part C runs the bootstrap by hand on the 76 test patients of the worked project. Part E reviews pieces of final projects written with an AI assistant, and Part F is the prompt workshop. The tab Check yourself holds seven questions, and the tab Reflect and export a reflection and the learning log. The data of Part C are public: UCI Heart Disease, Cleveland, CC BY 4.0.

Part A. The canvas of the final project

Write the question of your final project in its six parts. Name each input with the moment at which it is known, so that the canvas can ask whether it is known at the moment of the decision. The checks below the canvas are the questions that the jury asks first. The button Worked project fills in the canvas of the worked project of this week.







A participant proposes to predict a low ejection fraction from the ECG, with the inputs taken from the echocardiography report of the same day. What does the canvas flag first?

If the model should tell from an ECG who needs an echocardiogram, nothing of the echocardiogram can be an input. An input known only after the decision makes the model look better than it can be, and the question no longer matches the use.

Part B. How much data the model needs

Enter the number of candidate parameters of the model, the share of the outcome, the anticipated Cox-Snell R squared and the number of patients you have. A category with k levels counts as k − 1 parameters. The page gives the three sizes of Riley and colleagues, whether your data meet them, the shrinkage to expect with your data and the margin of the overall risk.

With 227 patients and ten parameters, criterion (i) expects a shrinkage of 0.895. What does this mean for the model?

A shrinkage of 0.895 says that the slope of the log-odds on new patients is expected near 0.9. The cross-validated calibration slope of the worked project, 0.892, agrees. The project reports it, or uses fewer parameters or a penalised model.

Part C. The bootstrap by hand

This part holds the 76 test patients of the worked project, with the probabilities of the reference (age, sex and chest pain type) and of the full model of eight variables. Draw resamples with replacement and watch the AUCs of both models and their difference, computed on the same resample. The histogram collects the differences, with the percentile interval and the share at or below zero. The size of the resample asks what more or fewer patients of the same kind would show.

The interval of the difference includes zero. Which conclusion is right?

An interval that includes zero shows neither a gain nor equivalence. With 304 patients drawn from the same 76, the interval narrows and may leave zero behind, which is a reason to plan a larger validation, not a result of this one.

Part E. Review of a final project

Each example below is a piece of a final project written with an AI assistant, or a prompt sent to one. Read it with the seven questions for reviewing a final project and choose its hidden problem.

1.

FEATURES = ["age", "sex", "cp", "trestbps", "chol", "thalach", "exang", "oldpeak", "ca", "thal"]   # to spare the angiography
print("test AUC", round(roc_auc_score(y_test, model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]), 3))   # 0.95
The vessels coloured by fluoroscopy and the thallium scan come from tests that follow the exercise test, so the referral does not have them yet. The higher AUC belongs to a later decision. Every input of a model must be known at the moment of the decision it informs.

2.

for cols in candidate_sets:
    auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, fit(cols).predict_proba(X_test[cols])[:, 1])
    best = max(best, (auc, cols))      # the variables chosen by their test AUC
Choosing the variables by their test AUC makes the test part of the training: The best of many tries on 76 patients looks better than it will be on new patients. The choices are made on the training patients, with cross-validation, and the test is opened once.

3.

X = pd.get_dummies(heart[ALL_COLUMNS], columns=["cp", "restecg", "slope", "thal"], dtype=int)
X = X.join(pd.get_dummies(heart["age"] // 5, prefix="age_band"))   # 30 columns for 104 patients with disease
Thirty parameters for 104 patients with disease give about three events per parameter, far below what criterion (i) of Riley and colleagues asks for. The model will overfit, and its probabilities will be too extreme on new patients. Fewer parameters, or a penalised model, fit the data.

4.

print("Our model reaches a test AUC of", round(roc_auc_score(y_test, p_model), 3), "and is ready for the clinic.")
An AUC alone says nothing about the gain: The age, the sex and the chest pain type alone reach 0.900 on the same patients. A model is judged against what the clinic already knows, on the same test patients.

5.

sensitivity = tp / (tp + fn)
print(f"The rule-out is safe: sensitivity {sensitivity:.0%}")      # 35 of 35
A sensitivity of 100 percent from 35 patients has a Wilson interval from 0.901 to 1; in the 7 women with disease, the lower bound falls to 0.646. Without the interval, the sentence claims a safety that the data cannot show.

6.

threshold = max(t for t in grid if (p_model[y_test == 1] >= t).all())   # the highest threshold that misses no test patient
A threshold set so that no test patient is missed fits the chance of these 76 patients, and the test sensitivity of 1 is then guaranteed, not measured. The threshold is chosen on the cross-validated training patients, and the test judges it once.

Part F. Prompt workshop

On the prompt path, a prompt takes the place of the code. Choose an exercise of the Jupyter notebook, write the seven fields of the prompt card in clinical language and watch the quality meter and the privacy check. The prompt names only the table "data" that the exercise cell prepares and the result "answer". Then copy the prompt into an AI assistant, paste the code it returns into the exercise cell in place of the line answer = None, and let the last line of the cell check the result.

    A weak and a strong prompt for the same task
    Both prompts ask whether the full model is better than the reference on the test patients. The code under each prompt is an example of what an AI assistant may return.
    Weak prompt
    is my new model better than the old one
    Example of code an assistant may return:
    auc_old = roc_auc_score(data["disease"], data["probability, reference"])
auc_new = roc_auc_score(data["disease"], data["probability, model"])
print("the new model is better:", auc_new > auc_old)      # True, with no uncertainty
    Strong prompt, with the prompt card
    Role: You are helping a cardiologist who does not write code. The code will be pasted into a Jupyter notebook and run there.
Context: The cardiologist wants a single number for how often, over the resamples, the full model fails to beat the reference.
Data: The notebook holds a table named "data" with the 76 test patients of the Cleveland data of the UCI Heart Disease data set, patients referred for coronary angiography after an exercise test. Each row is a patient: "patient" gives the number of the patient in the data, "probability, reference" the probability of coronary artery disease from a reference model of the course (a logistic regression on the age, the sex and the chest pain type), and "probability, model" the probability from the full model of the course (the same with the resting blood pressure, the cholesterol and three results of the exercise test added). Both models were fitted on the 227 training patients only. "disease" is 1 when the angiogram showed more than 50 percent narrowing of the diameter of at least one major coronary artery, and 0 otherwise.
Task: Draw 2,000 resamples of the test patients: Each resample draws as many patients as the table has, with replacement, with a random number generator started with the seed 2026. Skip a resample that holds patients of one outcome only. In each resample, compute the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the reference and of the model on the same patients, and the difference, model minus reference. The 95 percent interval of a result runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of its values over the resamples. Compute the share of the resamples in which the difference is 0 or less.
Constraints: Use only this table. The code must not read files, download anything from the internet or install programs.
Output: Print the number of resamples and the share, and keep the share, with three decimals, under the name "answer".
Test: The share must lie between 0 and 1, and the same resamples must serve both models.
    Example of code an assistant may return:
    y = data["disease"].to_numpy()
a, b = data["probability, reference"].to_numpy(), data["probability, model"].to_numpy()
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
diff = []
for _ in range(2000):
    i = rng.integers(0, len(y), len(y))
    if y[i].min() < y[i].max():
        diff.append(roc_auc_score(y[i], b[i]) - roc_auc_score(y[i], a[i]))
answer = round(float(np.mean(np.array(diff) <= 0)), 3)
print(len(diff), "resamples | interval", np.round(np.percentile(diff, [2.5, 97.5]), 3), "| not better:", answer)

# test: the share lies between 0 and 1
print(0 <= answer <= 1)
    Which fields of the strong prompt prevent the error of the weak one?
    The weak code compares two numbers and calls the larger one better, without any uncertainty. The strong prompt asks for the difference in each resample of the same patients and for the share in which it is zero or less, which is the honest answer to the question.

    Check yourself
    Seven questions for self-assessment. They are not graded.
    1. Which part of a structured question decides whether an input may be used?
    An input that is known only after the decision would not be there when the model is used. The time of the decision is the test for every input.
    2. The share of the outcome is 0.5. How many patients does criterion (iii) of Riley and colleagues ask for, with a margin of 0.05?
    (1.96/0.05)² × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1,536.64 × 0.25 = 384.2, rounded up to 385 patients. The share of 0.5 asks for the most patients.
    3. A model finds all 20 patients with disease of a test set. What is the lower bound of the 95 percent Wilson interval of its sensitivity?
    With k = n, the lower bound is n/(n + 1.96²) = 20/23.84 = 0.839. Twenty patients without a single miss still leave room for a sensitivity of 0.84.
    4. In the bootstrap of two models, why is the difference computed in each resample instead of from the two separate intervals?
    The paired difference uses the fact that both models see the same patients. Its interval answers the question of the gain; two overlapping intervals do not.
    5. A model has a calibration intercept of 0.4 in a new clinic and a slope of 1.0. What should be done before it is used there?
    An intercept above 0 says that disease is more common than the model predicts, as when the share of disease differs between two clinics. Updating the intercept corrects it; the threshold must then be checked again.
    6. Which study comes right after a silent run that went well?
    In a silent run nobody sees the output. The next question is what happens when clinicians see it. It covers safety, usability and human factors at small scale, the stage of DECIDE-AI.
    7. Which sentence belongs in the report of the worked project?
    The sentence gives each result with its interval and its reference, and claims no more than the data show. The other three claim a gain, a safety and an equality that the intervals do not support.

    Weekly project
    What to do. Finish the final project described on the final project page, with the steps of the worked project of this week. State the question with its population, input, output, decision and time; audit the data; check their size with the criteria of Riley and colleagues; compare the model with a simple reference; give every result with an interval; check the calibration; if the model informs a decision, choose its threshold on the training data only; report what the model uses and where it may fail; and write a plan for the next stage of its clinical evaluation. Present the project in the session in five to seven minutes.
    Where to look. Lecture: the whole page, in particular "A reference first", "Uncertainty: The bootstrap", "Reporting with TRIPOD+AI" and "Designing the clinical evaluation". Interactive lab: Parts A to D. Jupyter notebook: Part 1, Stops 1 to 3, or Part 2, Sections 1 to 10, and the starting points of the options at the end of Part 2.
    Prompt path. The final project can also be built with the prompts of the prompt library: four prompts, one for each step, whose data fields are rewritten for the data of your own project. The test of each prompt must hold before the next prompt is used, and no prompt ever holds data of real patients.
    Example. On the 76 test patients, the reference of age, sex and chest pain type reaches an AUC of 0.900 [0.824, 0.959], and the model of eight variables 0.931 [0.872, 0.977]. The difference, 0.031 [−0.020, 0.094], includes zero: On these data the model is not clearly better than the reference. At the rule-out threshold of 0.18, chosen on the training patients, the model rules out 27 of the 76 test patients without missing any of the 35 with disease. The sensitivity of 1 has the interval [0.901, 1.000], and the negative predictive value of 1 the interval [0.875, 1.000].
